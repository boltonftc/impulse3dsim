The Turret: Gears, Servos and Aiming

A turret lets the shooter swing left and right to aim, without turning the whole robot. Our turret is driven by a servo, through two sets of gears. In this lesson you'll learn how gear ratios work, why a servo can't spin forever (and what we do about it), how to turn a servo position into a turret angle, and how to drive the turret by hand with the D-pad — the first step toward letting the robot aim itself.

Not every robot has a turret, but everyone takes this lesson: gears, servos and limits show up on every robot you'll ever build. Like the last lesson, you won't change any code here.

1. The parts

Our turret uses three goBILDA® parts, chained together: the servo turns the overdrive gearbox, and the gearbox turns the turret.

2. Gear ratios

When two gears mesh, their teeth pass each other one for one. So if a small gear with 64 teeth drives a big gear with 176 teeth, the small gear has to turn 176 ÷ 64 = 2.75 times to make the big gear turn once. That number is the gear ratio, written 2.75:1.

Speed and torque always trade off
  • Reduction (small gear drives big gear, like 2.75:1): the output turns slower and with more torque (twisting strength).
  • Overdrive (big gear drives small gear, like 1:3.2): the output turns faster and farther, with less torque.
  • When gears are chained, you multiply (or divide) their ratios.
  • Two meshing gears spin in opposite directions — so a turret may turn the “wrong” way until you flip it in code.

3. Why a servo can't spin forever

A positional servo has a sensor inside that knows its own angle. You tell it a position with setPosition() and it drives there and holds it. But that sensor only covers a limited range: the Axon MAX can turn about 350°, and then it stops. That's the price of knowing exactly where it is.

A continuous-rotation servo (like the indexer's CRServo) can spin forever, but it gives up knowing where it is — you control its speed, not its angle. For aiming we need an angle, so the turret servo must be in positional (servo) mode.

Here's the problem. The turret's 2.75:1 gears divide the servo's motion. With the servo alone, the turret could only turn 350° ÷ 2.75 ≈ 127° — barely a third of a circle.

That's why we need the overdrive

The 1:3.2 overdrive multiplies the servo's 350° to 350 × 3.2 = 1,120° — more than three full turns. Then the turret's 2.75:1 gears bring it back down to 1,120 ÷ 2.75 ≈ 407° of turret travel: more than a full circle, from a servo that can't even turn once.

4. From servo position to turret angle

setPosition() takes a number from 0.0 to 1.0. Across that whole range, the turret swings through its full travel. One catch: the servo's full 350° needs a signal range the Control Hub doesn't send by default. With the hub's normal servo signal, you get about 90% of it — about 367° of turret travel. That's still more than a full circle, so we use that number.

Turret angle from servo position

turret angle = (position − 0.5) × 367°

  • 0.5 → 0° (straight forward)
  • 0.6 → +37° (a bit to the left)
  • 0.25 → −92° (pointing right)

And going the other way, to aim at an angle: position = 0.5 + angle ÷ 367°.

What about strength? The overdrive trades away torque: the Axon MAX's 34 kg·cm becomes about 11 kg·cm after the gearbox. The turret's 2.75:1 reduction then multiplies it back up to about 30 kg·cm at the turret — plenty to swing a shooter.

5. Setting it up safely

Before the turret ever moves
  • Program the servo first. Use the Axon Servo Programmer to make sure it's in servo (positional) mode with its full travel. In continuous mode, setPosition() can't hold an angle.
  • Center, then assemble. Send the servo to 0.5 first, then mesh the gears with the turret pointing straight forward. Now 0.5 means “forward” and you have equal travel each way.
  • It moves on INIT. A positional servo jumps to its commanded position as soon as the code starts. Keep hands clear of the turret when you press INIT.
  • Protect the wires. More than a full circle of travel can wrap and pull cables. Limit the range in code (for example 0.15 to 0.85, about ±128°) until the wiring is proven.
  • Give it power. An Axon MAX can draw up to 4 A when it's straining. Use a servo power injector so it doesn't brown out the hub.

6. Driving it by hand with the D-pad

For testing and manual override, the driver nudges the turret with the D-pad: hold left to swing left, right to swing right. In the simulator, the , and . keys (the < and > keys) do the same thing.

This works like the Hood's aiming, with one difference. The Hood used a stick, so pushing it farther moved it faster. A D-pad button is just on or off, so while it's held, the turret moves the same small step every loop. At dozens of loops a second, those small steps add up to a smooth swing. And every step is clamped to the safe limits, so the driver can't wrap the wires.

The turret is just another subsystem

Same shape as the Hood: a position field remembers where we want the turret, command methods (nudgeLeft(), nudgeRight(), setAngle()) only change that field, and update() sends it to the servo every loop. Nothing moves until robot.update() runs.

The simulator now has a servo named turret on the robot, so once you add the code, you'll see the turret swing — and shots fly where it points.

7. What's next: let the robot aim itself

Aiming by hand is slow. The real goal is a robot that keeps its turret pointed at the goal on its own. That's why the reward includes a setAngle(degrees) method: any part of your code can say “point at 30°”, and the turret does the math. Two ways to work out that angle are coming in a future lesson:

  • With the Limelight camera: it spots the goal's AprilTag and reports how many degrees off-center it is. Turn the turret by that much, and it's centered.
  • With odometry: the Pinpoint already knows where the robot is on the field. Since you also know where the goal is, a little trigonometry (Math.atan2) gives the angle to it — minus the robot's own heading.
