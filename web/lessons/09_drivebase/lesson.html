From Messy Loop to Clean Class

Your robot already drives. You taught it to strafe and spin back in the mecanum lesson, and last lesson you added the IMU so it knows which way it's pointing. So this lesson is not about making it move — it's about making your code clean enough to keep growing.

Open MecanumDrive.java and look at the run loop. Read it slowly. In those few lines, the loop is already juggling three completely different jobs at once:

  • reading the IMU heading (and re-zeroing it on the bumper),
  • doing all the drive math — four wheel powers, then normalizing them,
  • reading the gamepad and printing telemetry.

Right now that's about 25 lines crammed together. But a real competition robot also has an intake, a shooter, an arm, a climber… If every mechanism dumped its code into this one loop, you'd end up with 300+ lines of tangled math. Nobody — not even you — could read it, and one tiny typo could break everything.

The big idea: a class is a room

Think of your program as a house. Right now everything happens in one crowded room. The fix is to give each job its own room with a label on the door.

In Java, a room like that is called a class. The Drivebase class is the room where everything about driving lives — the four motors and the math that powers them. Nothing outside that room needs to know how driving works. They just knock on the door and say “drive!”

A class is made of two kinds of things:

  • Fields — the stuff the room owns. For Drivebase, that's the four motors.
  • Methods — the things the room can do. For Drivebase, that's driveFieldCentric(…) and stop().

There's also one special method called the constructor. It runs once, the moment you build the object — like unpacking a new appliance and plugging it in. Drivebase's constructor is where it finds its four motors in the hardwareMap and gets them ready.

Why public and private?

Look at the motors in Drivebase: they're marked private. The drive method is marked public. That's on purpose.

  • public = the door everyone is allowed to use. Your OpMode calls driveFieldCentric(…) through this door.
  • private = the wiring inside the wall. Only Drivebase is allowed to touch its own motors.

Why hide the motors? Because if any part of your program could reach in and set a motor power, a bug could come from anywhere, and you'd have no idea where to look. By locking the motors inside, the only way to drive is through the one door you control — so if driving misbehaves, you know exactly where the problem is.

And here's the payoff, the reason pro teams write code this way:

  • Teamwork. Once driving lives in its own room, one teammate can improve the drive while another builds the intake — in different files, without stepping on each other.
  • Reuse. Both your TeleOp and your Autonomous can say robot.drive.driveFieldCentric(…). Write it once, use it everywhere.
  • Easy to fix. Drive acting weird? You already know which room to walk into.

The three new files

Notice that a few new files just appeared in your project. Peek at each — you don't have to change them yet, just get a feel for them:

  • Drivebase.java — the drive room. You'll finish it in a minute.
  • Robot.java — think of it as the whole building. It builds each room (subsystem) for you, so your OpMode can just say new Robot(hardwareMap) once and get everything.
  • Subsystem.java — a shared rule every room agrees to follow (we'll dig into what an “interface” is a couple lessons from now).

Part A — Give the drive math its permanent home

Open Drivebase.java and find the drive method. The sticks are already read for you into three numbers — this is the same mecanum math you wrote inline before, except now it's moving into the room where it belongs.

▸ Drivebase.java : Drivebase : Loop
  • drive — forward / backward (left stick up/down)
  • strafe — sideways (left stick left/right)
  • turn — spin (the triggers)

Each wheel is just forward ± strafe ± turn. Adding three numbers can push a wheel past 1.0 (full power), so we find the largest and divide them all by it — that keeps every wheel's ratio (and the robot's direction) intact.

Type this into Drivebase.java⩢ Copy
double fl = forward + right + turn;
double fr = forward - right - turn;
double bl = forward - right + turn;
double br = forward + right - turn;
double max = Math.max(1.0, Math.max(Math.max(Math.abs(fl), Math.abs(fr)),
                                    Math.max(Math.abs(bl), Math.abs(br))));
frontLeft.setPower(fl / max * DRIVE_SPEED_SCALE);
frontRight.setPower(fr / max * DRIVE_SPEED_SCALE);
backLeft.setPower(bl / max * DRIVE_SPEED_SCALE);
backRight.setPower(br / max * DRIVE_SPEED_SCALE);

The room is finished — but nobody has knocked on its door yet. If you built and drove right now, nothing would change, because your OpMode is still doing the old inline math. Let's fix that.

Part B — Knock on the door (watch the loop collapse)

This is the satisfying part. Go back to MecanumDrive.java and replace the crowded code with calls to your new room. Two small swaps.

1. In INIT: build the robot instead of four loose motors

▸ MecanumDrive.java : Drive : Setup

Under the Drive : Setup comment you'll see four DcMotor lines plus two setDirection lines. Delete all six — the Drivebase room owns the motors now — and replace them with a single line that builds the whole robot:

Replace the six motor lines with this⩢ Copy
// Build the whole robot. Every subsystem's constructor runs here (finding its motors and
// servos), so it must happen during INIT, before the match starts.
Robot robot = new Robot(hardwareMap);

2. In the loop: hand the driving off to the drivebase

▸ MecanumDrive.java : Drive : Loop

Under the Drive : Loop comment is the whole block of drive math — the drive/strafe/turn lines, the four wheel powers, the normalize step, and the four setPower calls. Delete that entire block and replace it with just two calls:

Replace the whole drive block with this⩢ Copy
// 1) SCHEDULER: give every mechanism a turn to advance its own work.
robot.update();

// 2) DRIVE: hand the gamepad and the IMU heading to the drivebase.
robot.drive.driveFieldCentric(gamepad1, heading);

Look at what just happened: about 25 lines of math shrank to two clean sentences. The loop now reads like what it does — “update everything, then drive” — and the messy details are tucked safely inside the Drivebase room. That's OOP. That's the whole point.
