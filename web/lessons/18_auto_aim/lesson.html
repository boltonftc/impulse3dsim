Auto-Aim: A Turret That Never Looks Away

Before you start: finish the Turret lesson first

Auto-aim builds on the Turret lesson (The Turret: Gears, Servos and Aiming, just above this one). You need its Turret.java and the turret line in Robot.java, plus the Pinpoint Localization from the Advanced lessons, so the robot knows where it is. Haven't done the Turret lesson yet? Go back and do that one first.

Ever used a compass in Minecraft? Run anywhere, spin around, jump off a cliff — the needle keeps pointing at your spawn point. Lots of games have the same thing: a waypoint arrow that always points at the next objective, no matter which way you turn. By the end of this lesson your turret will be that arrow: the robot drives, spins and wiggles all over the field, and the shooter stays pointed at the hive. Nobody has to aim. The driver drives; the robot aims.

Here's the surprising part: the compass doesn't see your spawn point. The game simply knows where you are and where spawn is, and works out the direction between them. Our robot can't see the hive either (there's no camera in this lesson), but it knows the same two things:

  • Where it is: the Pinpoint has been tracking that since the localization lesson.
  • Where the hive is: it doesn't move, so we measure it once and write it down.

Turning those two facts into a turret angle takes a little math. Not all of trigonometry — just three ideas, one at a time.

1. The field is graph paper

A compass only works if everyone agrees where things are. So we lay a sheet of graph paper over the field, seen from where the red drivers stand:

  • (0, 0) is the center of the field.
  • +X points to the drivers' right.
  • +Y points away from the drivers, toward the far wall.
  • Distances are in inches. The field is 144″ across, so every wall is 72″ from the center.

The red hive has two cells. On our graph paper they're at:

  • Left cell: (−11.4, −12.75)
  • Right cell: (10.2, −12.75)

(These come from the simulator's field model. On a real field, measure them with a tape measure before you trust them.) Teams usually shoot from along the left or right wall, and the robot aims at the cell on its own side of the field: left side, left cell; right side, right cell.

2. Angles: a protractor on the field

Now imagine a giant protractor lying on the field. Every direction gets a number, and the rules are the same everywhere:

The angle rules
  • 0° points along +X (to the drivers' right).
  • Angles grow counter-clockwise: 90° is straight away from the drivers, 180° is left, and −90° is toward the drivers.
  • The robot's heading is just the angle its front is pointing.
  • The turret uses the same rule: + is left (counter-clockwise), − is right (clockwise). That's why lesson 17 said “+ = left”.

Why counter-clockwise? Mathematicians have measured angles that way for hundreds of years, so every math tool (including Java's Math class) expects it.

3. Idea #1: the arrow to the target

In Minecraft you might tell a friend “the base is 10 blocks east and 20 blocks north of you.” Our robot does the same thing: to get from the robot to the cell, how far do you go over, and how far up?

  • dx — how far over: dx = cell x − robot x
  • dy — how far up: dy = cell y − robot y

Always target minus robot: the arrow starts at the robot and ends at the cell. Do it backwards and the arrow points the wrong way!

4. Idea #2: atan2, the direction machine

We have “over” and “up”. We want a direction — an angle on the protractor. Java has a machine for exactly that: Math.atan2(dy, dx). Think of it like a vending machine: put in “how far up” and “how far over”, and out pops the direction. You don't need to know how the gears inside work. (It answers in radians, so we wrap it in Math.toDegrees.)

Why atan2 and not the tan⁻¹ button on a calculator? The calculator only looks at one number (dy ÷ dx), so it can't tell “up and right” from “down and left”. The “2” in atan2 means it looks at both numbers and their signs, so it always knows which way the arrow really points. Its answer is always between −180° and +180°.

5. Idea #3: field angle minus heading = turret angle

atan2 tells us the direction on the field. But the turret doesn't measure from the field's +X. It measures from the robot's front. That's like the difference between “the hive is to the northeast” and “the hive is a little to your right.” To turn one into the other, you need to know which way the robot is facing: its heading.

The one formula

turret angle = field angle − robot heading

One last tidy-up: wrapping

Angles go around in a circle, like a clock. On a clock, 13 o'clock is the same as 1 o'clock. On our protractor, 340° and −20° point the same way — and turning 20° right is a lot quicker than turning 340° left. So if the subtraction lands outside −180°…180°, we wrap it:

  • More than 180°? Subtract 360°. (340° → −20°)
  • −180° or less? Add 360°. (−200° → 160°)

8. Now do it 50 times a second

That took you a minute or so per shot. Here's the cool part: the robot does the exact same steps — dx, dy, atan2, subtract the heading, wrap — every single time through the run loop. That's about 50 times every second.

Think about what that means. Every 20 milliseconds the Pinpoint reports a fresh position and heading, the math runs, and the turret gets a fresh angle. The driver can race along the wall, spin the robot around, get bumped by another robot — and 50 times a second, the turret quietly corrects itself. It never looks away from the hive. That's exactly how the compass in a game works: every frame, the game re-does the math, so the needle always points home. 🧭

The big idea

A small piece of math, run over and over in the loop, turns into a behavior: “always point at the hive.” You never write “follow the hive” anywhere in the code. It happens because the answer is recalculated faster than the robot can move.

See it in action

Here's that loop running. When nobody's touching it, the robot drives itself around on autopilot. But it's interactive: grab the robot and drag it anywhere on the field, or slide its heading, and you're in control. Let go for a few seconds and autopilot takes over again. Watch the box: the field angle, minus the heading, gives the turret angle, recalculated every time anything moves. The orange turret never leaves the dashed line to the cell.

Things to try
  • Turn the robot without moving it. The field angle stays the same. Only the turret angle changes. That's the subtraction at work.
  • Drive along the side wall. Drag the robot up and down the right wall and watch the turret track the cell the whole way.
  • Creep across the middle. Drag the robot slowly from the right side into the grey strip. The target stays on the right cell until you come out the other side. (More on that in section 9.)
  • Spin the robot all the way around. Drag the heading slider slowly from one end to the other. The turret goes past 180° into the yellow band behind the robot, then, at 200°, unwinds. (Section 11 explains why.)

Making it real

The math is the heart of it. But a real robot on a real field needs a few more details before it works. Each one is a small idea of its own.

9. Which cell? The one on your side

The robot aims at the cell on the same side of the field as the robot. The line down the middle, from the red drivers through the center of the hive, is x = 0:

  • Robot on the left (x is negative) → aim at the left cell.
  • Robot on the right (x is positive) → aim at the right cell.

The hive is a seesaw, so sometimes the cell on your side is tipped down. That's fine. The turret still points at it while you collect pollen, and the driver simply doesn't shoot. When the driver wants a shot, they drive to the side with the raised cell, and the turret swings over to it on its own. No buttons needed.

The dead zone

What if the robot sits right on the line? Odometry wobbles by a fraction of an inch, so x would flip between +0.1 and −0.1, and the turret would whip back and forth between the two cells. To stop that, there's a dead zone: a strip 6″ wide on each side of the line.

Dead zone: inside it, change nothing
  • x more than +6 → right cell.
  • x less than −6 → left cell.
  • Anywhere in between → keep the cell you already had.

To switch, the robot has to come all the way out the other side of the strip. A home thermostat works the same way: it doesn't flip the heat on and off every time the temperature wiggles by a tenth of a degree.

The robot starts at x = 0, right in the dead zone, so auto-aim needs a first choice. It starts on the right cell, because that's the one raised at the start of the match.

10. Opening up the turret: ±200°

In lesson 17 we limited the turret to about ±128° to protect the wires. That leaves a blind spot behind the robot. But remember the math from lesson 17: through the overdrive and the turret gears, the servo's full signal gives about 407° of turret travel. That's more than a full circle, so the turret can point anywhere, even straight back over the robot.

Two changes unlock it, and they're both in the reward's new Turret.java:

  • Ask for the full signal. The Control Hub normally sends 600–2400 µs, which only reaches about 367°. One line asks for the servo's full 500–2500 µs: servo.setPwmRange(new PwmControl.PwmRange(500, 2500)); Now DEGREES_PER_FULL_RANGE is 407.
  • Widen the limits to ±200°. That's 400° of travel, just inside the 407° the hardware can do.
⚠ Check the wires first

±200° means the cables to the shooter twist through 400°. Before auto-aim is allowed to swing that far on a real robot:

  • Give the cables a service loop (or a cable carrier) long enough to follow the turret from −200° to +200° without pulling tight.
  • Make sure nothing can snag: no zip-tie tails, no loose wires near the gears.
  • Test it slowly first with setAngle(180) and setAngle(-180), watching the wires, before you let auto-aim drive it.

11. Behind the robot: two ways to point the same way

Since the turret can go past 180°, some directions can be reached two ways. Pointing straight back-left could be +190° (turn left, a little past halfway) or −170° (turn right, almost halfway). Both point the same way. That overlap is the yellow band in the widget: the 40° slice straight behind the robot, 20° each side of dead-back.

The “two ways” rule

When there are two ways to point at the cell, use the one that's the shorter swing from where the turret is now — as long as it stays within ±200°.

Why does this matter? Picture the cell slowly passing behind the robot. With only one way to point (−180° to 180°), the moment the answer crossed 180° it would jump to −180°, and the turret would whip all the way around even though the cell barely moved. With the two-ways rule, the turret simply keeps going a little past 180°. The 40° of overlap gives it room, so a cell wobbling behind the robot never makes it flip.

Unwinding

What if the driver keeps spinning the robot the same direction, around and around? The turret follows to 200°, and then there's only one way left. It has to unwind: swing all the way around to the other side and carry on from there. Like a phone cord that's twisted as far as it goes, the only fix is to untwist it. The turret is fast (about half a second for the full swing), so this costs a moment once in a while — never a missed direction.

All-Star practice

Try the hardest rounds: the cell is behind the robot, so you'll have to pick which way around to send the turret. Pick the shorter swing!

12. Trim: the driver's fine-tune

Odometry is good, but not perfect. The wheels slip a little, the cell numbers might be off by an inch, and a shot can drift. So the driver gets a trim: push the right stick left or right to shift the aim a little.

This is different from lesson 17's D-pad nudge. The nudge moved the turret itself. But auto-aim sets the turret 50 times a second, so a nudge would be wiped out right away. Instead, the trim is an offset that auto-aim adds on top of its answer:

turret angle = field angle − heading + trim

  • The trim stays where the driver leaves it, all match.
  • It's limited to ±20°, so a bumped stick can't send the aim somewhere silly.
  • The right stick's up/down still trims the flywheel speed, like before.

Our robot doesn't have a hood, so the right stick's left/right is free for the trim. (If your code still has a hood line on the right stick from an older version of this course, delete it.) And since auto-aim moves the turret now, the D-pad is free.

13. Where am I? Starting and staying found

A game's compass only works because the game always knows where you are. Our robot has to earn that. The Pinpoint resets to (0, 0) facing 0° every time an OpMode starts — it has no idea where on the field it is until somebody tells it. There are three ways that happens.

1. Auto leaves a note for TeleOp

Autonomous knows exactly where the robot is the whole time: it's driving paths in field coordinates. So every loop, it writes the robot's position on the blackboard, a place in the FTC software that survives from one OpMode to the next. It's like leaving a note on the fridge: “I parked at (−48, 16), facing 90°.” Right after START, TeleOp reads the note and calls setPose with it, and auto-aim works from the very first second.

// in auto, every loop:
blackboard.put("autoPose", new double[] { p.getX(), p.getY(), Math.toDegrees(p.getHeading()) });

// in TeleOp, right after START:
Object fromAuto = blackboard.remove("autoPose");
if (fromAuto instanceof double[]) {
    double[] p = (double[]) fromAuto;
    robot.localization.setPose(p[0], p[1], p[2]);
}
  • Auto writes the note every loop, not just at the end, because the match timer can stop auto partway through a path.
  • TeleOp uses remove, which reads the note and throws it away. If you stop and restart TeleOp later in practice, it won't trust an old note from a robot that may have been carried somewhere else.
  • Autos saved by the simulator's Path Capture tool already write the note. A hand-written auto needs the one line above.
  • Wait until after START to call setPose. The Pinpoint spends a moment calibrating after INIT, and a pose sent during that moment can get erased.

2. Nobody told us? Don't guess

If there's no note (you ran TeleOp by itself, or the robot was restarted), the robot doesn't know where it is. A guess would be worse than nothing: the turret would aim confidently at the wrong spot, and the driver might shoot. So auto-aim points the turret straight forward and the telemetry says “position unknown: park at HOME and hold RB.”

3. HOME: a reset button you can find by feel

HOME is one spot right in front of the drivers, next to the loading zone, that the robot can be put in exactly, without measuring:

  • Back against the drivers' wall — that pins down how far from the drivers it is (y).
  • Facing straight away from the wall — that pins down the heading: 90°.
  • Left side on the loading zone's right-hand tape line — that pins down x.

The position is the robot's center, so we add half the robot's size: with an 18″ × 18″ robot, HOME is at x = −22 + 9 = −13 and y = −71 + 9 = −62, facing 90°.

Hold RB for half a second at HOME

One button fixes both of the robot's “which way am I facing” answers at once:

  • It resets the IMU, so field-centric driving knows “forward” is away from the drivers (like before).
  • It tells the Pinpoint it's at HOME, so auto-aim knows exactly where the robot is.

At HOME both are true at the same moment, so one button can do both. The rule: only press RB at HOME. Press it anywhere else and the robot will believe it's at HOME when it isn't, and every shot will miss until you reset properly.

Why hold for half a second instead of a quick press? So a bumped button in the middle of a match can't move the robot's idea of where it is. The code uses a stopwatch (ElapsedTime) that restarts every time RB is up; only when it reaches 0.5 seconds does the reset happen.

HOME is also the fix for drift. Odometry is very good, but after two minutes of bumping into robots it can be off by an inch or two. Since the robot parks near the drivers anyway, a quick hold of RB at HOME wipes the slate clean. (Coming up in a future lesson: the Limelight camera can spot AprilTags on the field walls and correct the position automatically.)

4. Field-centric after auto: trust the Pinpoint, not the IMU

Here's a sneaky one. Field-centric driving needs to know which way the robot faces compared to the drivers. Until now it asked the Control Hub's IMU. But the IMU knows nothing about the field: it calls “forward” whichever way the robot faced when TeleOp started. If auto ends with the robot turned 45°, every push of the stick in TeleOp comes out 45° off.

The Pinpoint doesn't have that problem. Auto told it the real start pose, and the note on the blackboard carried the pose into TeleOp, so its heading is a true field heading. So once the pose is known, field-centric uses the Pinpoint's heading instead. Facing away from the red drivers is field heading 90°, and field-centric calls that 0°, so we subtract 90:

heading = Math.toRadians(robot.localization.getHeadingDegrees() - 90);

If the pose isn't known yet (TeleOp by itself, no HOME reset), it falls back to the IMU, just like before. And the HOME reset still lines both up: at HOME the Pinpoint says 90° (so this gives 0) and the IMU is reset to 0.

14. The code: your worksheet, in Java

Here's the heart of the AutoAim class you'll unlock. It's the same worksheet you filled in during practice, one step at a time. Let's walk through it, then see it all together. Along the way you'll meet four small Java tools that make it short: ? :, while, Math.abs and &&.

Before anything: don't guess

if (!localization.isPoseKnown()) {
    turret.setAngle(0);      // point straight ahead
    return;                  // and skip all the math below
}

The ! means “not”, so this reads: if the position is NOT known, face forward and stop here. return leaves the method early, so none of the steps below run until auto or a HOME reset tells the robot where it is (section 13).

Step 0: which cell?

if (robotX > DEAD_ZONE)  aimingRight = true;
if (robotX < -DEAD_ZONE) aimingRight = false;
double targetX = aimingRight ? RIGHT_CELL_X : LEFT_CELL_X;

The first two lines are the dead zone from section 9. Right of +6? Aim right. Left of −6? Aim left. In between, neither line runs, so aimingRight keeps whatever it already was.

New Java tool: the ? : shortcut

question ? answerIfYes : answerIfNo is a one-line if/else that gives back a value. So aimingRight ? RIGHT_CELL_X : LEFT_CELL_X means “if we're aiming right, use the right cell's x; otherwise use the left cell's x.” It's the same as:

double targetX;
if (aimingRight) targetX = RIGHT_CELL_X;
else             targetX = LEFT_CELL_X;

Steps 1 and 2: the arrow, and which way it points

double dx = targetX - robotX;                               // how far over
double dy = targetY - robotY;                               // how far up
double fieldAngle = Math.toDegrees(Math.atan2(dy, dx));     // which way is the arrow?

Exactly sections 3 and 4: target minus robot, then the atan2 machine. Math.atan2 answers in radians, so Math.toDegrees converts it to degrees, the unit the rest of our code uses.

Step 3: from the robot's front

double turretAngle = fieldAngle - localization.getHeadingDegrees() + trimDeg;

The one formula from section 5, plus the driver's trim from section 12. The heading comes from the Pinpoint, the same sensor that gave us robotX and robotY.

Step 4: wrap it into −180…180

turretAngle = wrapDegrees(turretAngle);

private static double wrapDegrees(double degrees) {
    while (degrees > 180)   degrees -= 360;
    while (degrees <= -180) degrees += 360;
    return degrees;
}

This is the wrapping rule from section 5, written as a little helper method so the main code stays readable.

New Java tool: while

A while loop repeats its line as long as the condition is true. So “while (degrees > 180) degrees -= 360;” means: still too big? Take away 360. Check again. Repeat until it isn't. (-= 360 is short for “subtract 360 from it”.)

  • 340 → more than 180, subtract 360 → −20. Done.
  • −324 (Example 2 in section 6) → the second loop adds 360 → 36.
  • 700 → 340 → −20. Two trips around! That's why it's a while and not just an if: it keeps going until the answer is in range, however far off it started.

Why > 180 on top but <= -180 on the bottom? 180° and −180° point the same way, so we pick just one of them (180) to keep. Otherwise a direction could have two answers.

Step 5: the other way around

double otherWay = (turretAngle > 0) ? turretAngle - 360 : turretAngle + 360;
double now = turret.getAngle();
if (Math.abs(otherWay) <= Turret.MAX_ANGLE
        && Math.abs(otherWay - now) < Math.abs(turretAngle - now)) {
    turretAngle = otherWay;
}

This is the “two ways” rule from section 11, one line at a time:

  1. Find the other way around. Every direction has a twin that's 360° away. If the answer is positive, its twin is 360 less; if it's negative, 360 more. That's the ? : shortcut again: −170 becomes +190, and +170 becomes −190.
  2. Where is the turret now? turret.getAngle() tells us where it's currently pointed.
  3. Is the twin worth using? Only if both of these are true:
    • The turret can reach it: no more than 200° either way.
    • It's a shorter swing from where the turret is now.
    If so, use the twin instead.
New Java tools: Math.abs and &&
  • Math.abs(x) is the size of a number, ignoring its sign: Math.abs(-190) is 190. So Math.abs(otherWay) <= 200 means “no more than 200° to the left or the right”, and Math.abs(otherWay - now) is how far the turret would swing to get there, whichever direction that is.
  • && means AND: the if only runs when the part before it and the part after it are both true.

Try it with numbers. The answer from step 4 is −170°, so the twin is +190°.

Turret is now at…Reachable? |190| ≤ 200Swing to +190Swing to −170Use
+175°yes|190 − 175| = 15|−170 − 175| = 345+190° (the twin)
−130°yes|190 + 130| = 320|−170 + 130| = 40−170° (keep it)

Same direction either way — the rule just picks the one that gets there fastest. And when the twin is past 200°, the first check fails, the turret has to take the only way left, and that's the unwind.

Step 6: aim!

turret.setAngle(turretAngle);

Hand the answer to the Turret from lesson 17. It turns degrees into a servo position, and the next robot.update() sends it to the servo.

All together

Here's the whole thing in one piece. This is the inside of AutoAim's aim() method. Every line is one row of your practice worksheet, and the robot runs all of it 50 times a second.

if (!localization.isPoseKnown()) {                                // don't guess
    turret.setAngle(0);
    return;
}
if (robotX > DEAD_ZONE)  aimingRight = true;                       // 0. which cell? (dead zone: no change)
if (robotX < -DEAD_ZONE) aimingRight = false;
double targetX = aimingRight ? RIGHT_CELL_X : LEFT_CELL_X;

double dx = targetX - robotX;                                     // 1. how far over
double dy = targetY - robotY;                                     //    how far up
double fieldAngle = Math.toDegrees(Math.atan2(dy, dx));           // 2. which way is the arrow?
double turretAngle = fieldAngle - localization.getHeadingDegrees() + trimDeg;   // 3. from the robot's front
turretAngle = wrapDegrees(turretAngle);                           // 4. keep it in -180..180

double otherWay = (turretAngle > 0) ? turretAngle - 360 : turretAngle + 360;   // 5. the other way around
double now = turret.getAngle();
if (Math.abs(otherWay) <= Turret.MAX_ANGLE
        && Math.abs(otherWay - now) < Math.abs(turretAngle - now)) {
    turretAngle = otherWay;                                       //    reachable AND a shorter swing
}
turret.setAngle(turretAngle);                                     // 6. aim!

wrapDegrees() isn't in this block because it's a separate little method. In Java you can't write one method inside another, so it lives just below aim() in AutoAim.java, and aim() calls it in step 4. You'll see both, plus the cell positions and other constants, in the complete file you unlock with the quiz.

Notice what it doesn't need: no sine, no cosine, no triangles to solve by hand. atan2 does the hard part, and the rest is subtraction.
